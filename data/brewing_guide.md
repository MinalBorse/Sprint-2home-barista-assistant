# Home Brewing Guide

This guide explains how to brew good coffee at home with the most common methods. For each method it gives the grind size, the coffee-to-water ratio, the water temperature, the brew time and the main steps. Use it as a starting point, then adjust to your taste.

## Water temperature for brewing

For most brewing methods, use water between 90 and 96 degrees Celsius. If you don't have a thermometer, boil the water and let it rest for about 30 seconds.

- Light roasts usually taste best with hotter water, around 94 to 96 degrees Celsius.
- Dark roasts usually taste best with slightly cooler water, around 88 to 92 degrees Celsius, which reduces bitterness.
- Water that is too cool can make coffee sour. Water that is boiling can make coffee bitter.

## Pour-over and V60 (filter or drip coffee)

A pour-over, such as the Hario V60, brews coffee by pouring hot water slowly over ground coffee in a paper filter.

- Grind: medium-fine, like table salt.
- Ratio: about 1:16, for example 15 grams of coffee for 240 grams of water.
- Water temperature: 92 to 96 degrees Celsius.
- Total brew time: about 2 minutes 30 seconds to 3 minutes 30 seconds.

Steps:
1. Rinse the paper filter with hot water, then throw that water away.
2. Add the coffee and pour about twice its weight in water to "bloom" it. Wait 30 to 45 seconds.
3. Pour the rest of the water slowly in small circles, keeping the coffee bed covered.
4. If it drains too fast, grind finer. If it drains too slowly, grind coarser.

## French press (cafetiere, press pot, coffee plunger)

A French press steeps coffee grounds in hot water, then separates them with a metal mesh filter. It makes a full-bodied cup.

- Grind: coarse, like sea salt.
- Ratio: about 1:15, for example 20 grams of coffee for 300 grams of water.
- Water temperature: 93 to 96 degrees Celsius.
- Steep time: about 4 minutes.

Steps:
1. Add the coffee and pour in all the water.
2. Stir gently after 1 minute to break the crust of grounds on top.
3. At 4 minutes, press the plunger down slowly.
4. Pour all the coffee out right away. Coffee left on the grounds keeps brewing and turns bitter.

## AeroPress

The AeroPress is a small plastic brewer that steeps coffee briefly and then pushes it through a paper filter with air pressure. It is quick, forgiving and easy to clean.

- Grind: medium-fine to fine.
- Ratio: about 1:14, for example 15 grams of coffee for 210 grams of water.
- Water temperature: 85 to 92 degrees Celsius. The AeroPress works well with slightly cooler water.
- Brew time: about 1 to 2 minutes, plus 20 to 30 seconds of pressing.

Steps:
1. Put a rinsed paper filter in the cap.
2. Add the coffee and the water, and stir for about 10 seconds.
3. Wait about 1 minute, then press gently and steadily.
4. Stop pressing when you hear a hissing sound.

## Moka pot (stovetop espresso maker)

A moka pot is a stovetop brewer that pushes hot water up through the coffee using steam pressure. It makes strong, intense coffee, but it is not true espresso.

- Grind: fine, but slightly coarser than espresso.
- Coffee: fill the basket level to the top. Do not press or tamp it down.
- Water: fill the bottom chamber up to just below the safety valve.

Steps:
1. Use hot water in the bottom chamber, so the coffee spends less time on the heat.
2. Brew on medium-low heat with the lid open.
3. When the coffee turns pale and starts to sputter or gurgle, take it off the heat.
4. Cool the base under cold water to stop brewing. Overheating a moka pot is the most common cause of bitter, burnt-tasting moka coffee.

## Espresso

Espresso is made by forcing hot water through finely ground, tightly packed coffee at high pressure, about 9 bar. It needs an espresso machine.

- Grind: fine, like flour or powdered sugar.
- Ratio: about 1:2, for example 18 grams of ground coffee to get 36 grams of espresso in the cup.
- Water temperature: 90 to 96 degrees Celsius.
- Shot time: about 25 to 30 seconds.

If the shot runs much faster than 25 seconds, it usually tastes sour: grind finer. If it runs much slower than 30 seconds, it usually tastes bitter: grind coarser.

## Cold brew

Cold brew steeps coarse coffee in cold or room-temperature water for many hours. It tastes smooth, sweet and low in acidity.

- Grind: coarse.
- Ratio: about 1:8 for a concentrate, for example 100 grams of coffee for 800 grams of water.
- Steep time: 12 to 24 hours, in the fridge or at room temperature.

Filter the coffee after steeping. Dilute the concentrate with water or milk, about one to one, before drinking.
## Percolator (stovetop or electric)

A percolator brews by pushing boiling water up a tube and showering it over the coffee again and again. It makes a strong, hot, full-bodied cup, but it can easily turn bitter.

- Grind: coarse, like sea salt. Fine grounds fall through the basket.
- Ratio: about 1:16, for example 30 grams of coffee for 500 ml of water.
- Heat: medium-low. A gentle "perk" every few seconds, not a rolling boil.
- Brew time: about 7 to 10 minutes after it starts to perk.

Steps:
1. Fill the bottom with water, up to the line below the basket.
2. Put the coarse coffee in the basket and close the lid.
3. Heat until it starts to perk, then turn the heat down.
4. Let it perk gently for 7 to 10 minutes, then take it off the heat.
5. Remove the basket of grounds before serving.

If it tastes bitter or burnt, the heat was too high or it perked too long. Percolators over-extract easily.

## South Indian filter coffee (filter kaapi)

South Indian filter coffee is made in a two-part steel filter. Hot water drips slowly through the coffee to make a strong concentrate called decoction, which is mixed with hot milk and sugar.

- Coffee: fine grind, often a blend of coffee and chicory (about 80 to 20).
- Ratio: about 20 grams of coffee for 100 ml of water, for a strong decoction.
- Water: just boiled.
- Brew time: about 10 to 15 minutes of slow dripping.

Steps:
1. Put the coffee in the upper chamber and place the pressing disc on top.
2. Pour in the hot water, cover, and let it drip into the lower chamber.
3. Mix 1 part decoction with 2 to 3 parts hot milk, and add sugar to taste.
4. Pour it between a tumbler and a dabara to make it frothy.

If the decoction is too weak, use more coffee. If it drips too slowly, don't press the disc down so hard.

## Siphon coffee (vacuum pot or syphon)

A siphon, also called a vacuum pot, brews coffee using vapour pressure and a vacuum. It looks like a science experiment and makes a very clean, aromatic cup.

- Grind: medium, a little finer than for a French press.
- Ratio: about 1:15, for example 20 grams of coffee for 300 ml of water.
- Water temperature: about 90 to 95 degrees Celsius in the top chamber.
- Brew time: about 1 minute of steeping, 3 to 4 minutes in total.

Steps:
1. Fill the bottom bowl with hot water and fit the top chamber with its filter.
2. Heat the bottom. Vapour pressure pushes the water up into the top chamber.
3. Add the coffee to the top chamber and stir gently.
4. Let it steep for about 1 minute, then turn off the heat.
5. As the bottom bowl cools, a vacuum pulls the coffee down through the filter.

If it tastes sour, grind finer or steep a little longer. If it tastes bitter, steep for less time.

## Vietnamese phin (drip filter)

A phin is a small metal drip filter that sits on top of a cup. It makes a strong, concentrated coffee, traditionally served with sweetened condensed milk.

- Grind: medium-fine, a little coarser than espresso.
- Ratio: about 1:5, for example 20 grams of coffee for 100 ml of water.
- Water temperature: just off the boil, about 92 to 96 degrees Celsius.
- Brew time: about 4 to 5 minutes of slow dripping.

Steps:
1. Put sweetened condensed milk in the cup, if you like.
2. Add the coffee to the phin, shake it level and press the insert down lightly.
3. Pour in a little hot water to bloom the coffee and wait 30 seconds.
4. Fill the phin with hot water, put the lid on and let it drip.
5. Stir in the milk, or pour it over ice for Vietnamese iced coffee.

If it drips through in under 3 minutes, grind finer or press the insert more firmly. If it barely drips, grind coarser.

## Turkish coffee (cezve or ibrik)

Turkish coffee is brewed by heating very finely ground coffee with water in a small pot called a cezve or ibrik. It is served unfiltered, so the grounds settle at the bottom of the cup.

- Grind: extra fine, like flour. Finer than espresso.
- Ratio: about 1:10, for example 7 grams of coffee for 70 ml of cold water.
- Heat: low and slow. It should never boil.
- Brew time: about 3 to 4 minutes.

Steps:
1. Add cold water, coffee and sugar (if you like) to the cezve, and stir well.
2. Heat slowly on low heat. Do not stir again once it is warm.
3. When a thick foam rises to the rim, take it off the heat before it boils.
4. Pour slowly into a small cup, keeping some foam on top.
5. Wait a minute for the grounds to settle, and don't drink the last sip.

If it tastes bitter or burnt, the heat was too high or it boiled.
